I Fell to My Lowest Point. One Email Helped Me Climb Out.

  • 22 October 2026
  • graduate school,
  • resilience,
  • research,
  • personal

All three of my final first-author papers from grad school have now passed 100 citations each, a milestone I owe to a lot of people who took a chance on me. Those papers almost never happened. Here’s my story.

It was May of 2019, and I was at an all time low. I stood in my parents’ driveway staring down my basketball hoop. I had made 13 of my last 19 free throws. One more make and I’d hit my goal. I hoisted the ball up aaaaand ... Clang! Miss. For the past month, I had walked out to this same mark in the concrete with the same goal of making 14 of 20 free throws; I came up empty for the 30th straight time. I had set this goal to prove to myself that I was redeemable, that I was a good-for-something. But, alas, it seemed I wasn’t.

Four months earlier, I had graduated from college with a 3.2 GPA, a low mark for someone hoping to get into a Ph.D. program. I'd spent the past year telling myself I'd never get in. I applied to ten schools anyway, some prestigious, some not, and moved back home to wait.

The rejections poured in, broken up by the occasional waitlist. One by one, the waitlists turned into rejections too, including the school where I'd earned my bachelor's. The one that stung most came from a professor I'd been corresponding with for a year. He messaged to ask if I'd like to join his lab. I told him I'd love to, but that the university hadn't admitted me, and asked if he could check on my status. I never heard from him again.

So there I was in the driveway: nine rejections, one waitlist I'd been sitting on for four months, and a pretty good idea of how it would end (hint: poorly). I went inside and called my uncle, who'd been through grad school in the '90s. "Just email the professor you want to work with," he said. "You have nothing to lose."

So I did. I emailed Dr. Rampi Ramprasad at Georgia Tech. To my surprise, he emailed me back, asking if I was free for a call the next week. I told him I’d be in Atlanta anyway (I wasn’t—I booked a flight that night). The rest is history; he gave me the opportunity to join his lab as a Ph.D. student, an opportunity I’ll forever be thankful for.

I never did make 14 of 20; it turned out I didn’t need to.

That fall, on the morning of my first day, I stepped onto my balcony and squinted at the sun glinting off the Midtown skyscrapers. I sipped my coffee and said, to the city as much as to myself:

“This is my moment. It’s time to show the world they were wrong.”
